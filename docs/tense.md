# Tenses

English tense is the combination of two dimensions: **time** (past, present, future) × **aspect** (simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive), which gives 16 cells in theory. I used to treat a common claim as a rule: English verbs **have no morphological future** — the *will* in *will do* is a modal, *be going to* is a phrase, so futurity is expressed with lexical means. Below is the full 16-cell picture first, then the nine tenses that carry the actual load; the key sentence of each tense reads aloud on tap.

## The 16-cell picture

| Time \ Aspect | Simple | Progressive | Perfect | Perfect progressive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | do / does | am / is / are doing | have / has done | have / has been doing |
| Past | did | was / were doing | had done | had been doing |
| Future | will do | will be doing | will have done | will have been doing |
| Past future | would do | would be doing | would have done | would have been doing |

Do not spread yourself evenly across all 16. Everyday use and the exam concentrate on **simple present, present progressive, simple past, past progressive, present perfect, past perfect, simple future, present perfect progressive and past future**; you only need to recognize the other seven. The "past future" row mostly shows up in reported speech and narration (see below).

## The nine tenses that matter

### Simple present

**Structure**: do / does (third person +s)

- Habit and routine: <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She gets up at six every day." text="She gets up at six every day." />
- Objective truth: Water boils at 100 degrees.
- Schedules and fixed arrangements (a future stated as settled by a timetable): <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The train leaves at nine tomorrow." text="The train leaves at nine tomorrow." />

Common errors: dropping the third-person *s*; **stative verbs (know, belong, love, want) are not used progressively** — *I am knowing* is wrong.

### Present progressive

**Structure**: am / is / are + doing

- Happening right now: <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Look! The baby is sleeping." text="Look! The baby is sleeping." />
- An ongoing stretch of time (not necessarily this instant): I am writing a book these days.
- With *always*, an emotional charge of complaint or praise: <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He is always losing his keys." text="He is always losing his keys." />

### Simple past

**Structure**: did (regular verbs +ed)

- An action that happened in the past and is **cut off from the present**: I saw him yesterday.
- Often paired with explicit past-time adverbials: *last year*, *in 2010*, *two days ago*. That is the root of its exclusivity with the present perfect.

### Past progressive

**Structure**: was / were + doing

- A background action in progress at a past moment: <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I was reading when the phone rang." text="I was reading when the phone rang." /> (*read* sets the background, *ring* is the interrupting event)
- Two parallel backgrounds joined by *while*: While she was cooking, he was setting the table.

### Present perfect

**Structure**: have / has + done

**The core idea is "something in the past leaves a result or a connection with now"**, so it speaks to the present and never appears in the same sentence as an explicit past time point:

- Result in the present: I have lost my keys. (I still have not found them.)
- Continuing to the present: We have lived here for ten years. (We still live here.)
- Experience (the list of a life so far): She has been to Japan twice.

Markers: *already*, *yet*, *just*, *ever*, *never*, *since*, *for*, *so far*, *recently*.
**The number one error**: it cannot be combined with an explicit past time point — *I have seen him **yesterday*** is wrong. Say either *I saw him yesterday* or *I have seen him **recently***. The rule of thumb: **if the sentence names a specific past time, use the simple past; if it names none and stresses a connection to now, use the present perfect.**

### Past perfect

**Structure**: had + done

"The past of the past" — looking back from a past moment at something earlier:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="By the time we arrived, the film had started." text="By the time we arrived, the film had started." /> (arrival is past, the start is earlier)
- Setting the scene in narrative: She told me she had finished the report.
- The inverted *hardly … when / no sooner … than* construction: Hardly had he sat down when the phone rang.

It does not stand alone: the past perfect needs a **past reference point**. Without one, use the simple past.

### Simple future (five ways to say it)

| Form | Emphasis | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| will do | spontaneous decisions, predictions, promises | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I will help you with the boxes." text="I will help you with the boxes." /> |
| be going to do | prior intention, a prediction with visible signs | Look at those clouds. It is going to rain. |
| present progressive | an arrangement already fixed | We are meeting the clients at ten. |
| simple present | timetables (see above) | The plane takes off at six. |
| be about to do | imminent | Hurry up! The show is about to start. |

How *will* and *be going to* divide up the work: an empty glass on the table prompts **I will get** some water (a decision made on the spot); **We are going to** move next month (decided in advance).

### Past future

**Structure**: would do / was going to do

Speaking of "later" from inside the past: the one-step backshift in reported speech (He said he **would come**), and "future that did not happen" in narrative: I was going to call you, but I forgot (I intended to, but did not).

### Present perfect progressive

**Structure**: have / has been + doing

An action that began at a point in the past and runs to the moment of speaking (and usually keeps running), carrying the "strain and quantity" of the process itself:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I have been waiting for two hours." text="I have been waiting for two hours." /> (two hours of waiting, and still waiting)
- Contrast with the result-reading of the present perfect: <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Someone has painted the wall." text="Someone has painted the wall. (the wall is painted — the result)" /> vs <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Someone has been painting the wall." text="Someone has been painting the wall. (painting all morning — the process)" />

## Three high-frequency contrasts

**Simple past vs present perfect** — it is a fight between "cut off" and "connected":

| | Simple past | Present perfect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timeline | a past point, the door is shut | happened in the past, the line runs to now |
| Typical adverbials | yesterday, in 2010, ago | already, yet, since, for |
| Example | I lost my keys yesterday. | I have lost my keys. (still can't find them) |

**have been to vs have gone to**: He has been to Paris (went and came back); He has gone to Paris (went and is not back yet). One is experience, the other is whereabouts.

**when vs while**: the action in a *when*-clause can be long, short or instantaneous (I was reading **when** he called); *while* takes a durative action, usually with the progressive (**While** she was cooking, …), and can also express contrast (I like tea **while** he likes coffee).

## Main future, subordinate present

In adverbial clauses of time (*when*, *once*, *until*, *as soon as*), condition (*if*, *unless*) and concession (*even if*), **the simple present stands in for the future**, which is what lets the main clause use *will*:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I will call you as soon as I arrive." text="I will call you as soon as I arrive." /> (not *as soon as I will arrive*)
- Unless it rains, the match will go on.

Know the three common exceptions: **if expressing willingness** — If you will follow me, please (a request); **whether … or not can take will** — I don't know whether it will rain or not (that is an object clause, not a conditional clause, so the rule does not apply); **present perfect in the conditional clause** marks a completed precondition (If you have finished, let's go).

## Tense concord (reported speech)

When the main verb is in the past, the whole object clause shifts back one step: simple present → simple past, present perfect / simple past → past perfect, will → would, can → could:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She said she was busy." text="She said she was busy." /> (original words: *I am busy.*)
- He told me he had seen the film. (original words: *I have seen the film.*)

Cases that **do not shift**: when the clause states an objective truth (The teacher said that water **boils** at 100 degrees.), or when the reported matter is still true now (She said she **is** coming tomorrow — tomorrow has not arrived, so *is* is still natural in context).
