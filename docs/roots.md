# Word roots

> This page records **where roots and affixes come from**: for each common root and affix, which language it came from, what it meant originally, and how it evolved step by step into today's words. For a quick-memorization breakdown chart, go to [Roots and affixes](/exam/roots-and-affixes); for derived word families, go to [Root families](/topic/root-families); for synonym replacements among high-frequency exam words, go to [High-frequency vocabulary](/exam/vocabulary).
>
> Every example word is tappable. Recognizing the parts is step one; reading the word out loud is step two to making it stick.

## Two layers of origin

The vocabulary of modern English looks like this in a classic count (Finkenstaedt & Wolff, 1973): French origin about 28%, Latin origin about 28%, Germanic origin about 25%, Greek origin about 6%. In other words, **half the words in an English dictionary trace back to Latin and French, with another tenth from Greek**. These two layers supply the bulk of academic and written vocabulary, and they are where root-and-affix analysis pays off most. The [History](/history) page explains how these borrowings happened; this page looks at the parts that were left behind.

## Latin roots (9)

### dict: to say (Latin dicere)

The Latin verb dicere means "to say, to speak", with the past participle dictus. Roman legal custom gave this root its most formal use: judges and juries "spoke out" their rulings, and verdict (vere + dict, truly spoken) is still a ruling today. Witnesses testified under oath, so testify and dictate (to dictate aloud) come from the same semantic field. The root reached English mainly through two channels: borrowing Latin written words directly (predict, indicate), and borrowing legal and governmental terms through French (verdict).

Examples: <SpeakButton word="predict" text="predict" /> <SpeakButton word="contradict" text="contradict" /> <SpeakButton word="verdict" text="verdict" />

### spect: to look (Latin specere)

Latin specere "to look", past participle spectus; the earlier Proto-Indo-European root \*spek- "to observe" grew into Greek skopein, which is where telescope and scope come from. The same "looking" left two branches, one Latin and one Greek. Romans liked watching the gladiators, and spectator specifically meant an audience member in the stands; circumspect (looking all around) extended from "looking about in every direction" to "cautious". inspect looks inward; respect (re- = looking again) keeps re-seeing in the eye.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="inspect" text="inspect" /> <SpeakButton word="respect" text="respect" /> <SpeakButton word="prospect" text="prospect" />

### port: to carry (Latin portare) and harbour (Latin portus)

The English port has one spelling but two sources. portare "to carry, to transport" gave the family transport, portable, export; portus "harbour, city gate" gave the harbour sense of port and portal (a gate). portus descends from the PIE root \*per- "to cross, to pass through", and is cognate with the native word ford. Vine harvests add one more port: Port wine, named after the Portuguese city of Porto — a full circle, and still inside the "harbour" family.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="transport" text="transport" /> <SpeakButton word="portable" text="portable" /> <SpeakButton word="portal" text="portal" />

### duc/duct: to lead (Latin ducere)

Latin ducere "to lead, to guide"; in the Roman army the man who led the troops was called dux, and the word later became a commander's title — Mussolini's title Il Duce was exactly that. The Latin ancestor of educate, educare "to rear, to train", belongs to the same family as educere "to lead out": the popular gloss is "to draw a person's potential out", a breakdown that holds in the educational context and also stands on etymological ground. introduce leads something in, reduce leads it back (extended to "lessen"), and conduct leads it along (extended to "guide").

Examples: <SpeakButton word="educate" text="educate" /> <SpeakButton word="introduce" text="introduce" /> <SpeakButton word="reduce" text="reduce" />

### scrib/script: to write (Latin scribere)

Latin scribere "to write", originally meaning to scratch on a wax tablet. The copyists in medieval monasteries were called scribes, and manuscript by manuscript (manu = hand + scriptum = what has been written) they replicated them line by line. Before printing existed, "writing" was itself a profession. describe writes things down, subscribe signs your name below the document (extended to "subscribe"), and postscript is the extra line added after the letter was already finished — the abbreviation P.S. is still used at the end of letters.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="describe" text="describe" /> <SpeakButton word="manuscript" text="manuscript" /> <SpeakButton word="subscribe" text="subscribe" />

### fer: to bring (Latin ferre)

The Latin irregular verb ferre "to bring, to bear", past participle latum. It has a distant relative: the PIE root \*bher- "to carry" grew in the Germanic branch into the native English word **bear** — fer and bear are one and the same word from six thousand years ago. transfer carries across, refer brings back (extended to "consult", "mention"), confer brings opinions together (extended to "confer"), and fertile means "able to bear fruit, able to bring forth".

Examples: <SpeakButton word="transfer" text="transfer" /> <SpeakButton word="refer" text="refer" /> <SpeakButton word="prefer" text="prefer" />

### ject: to throw (Latin iacere)

Latin iacere "to hurl, to throw", past participle iactus. project throws forward (extended to "scheme", "projection"), inject throws into the body, and reject throws back (extended to "reject"). The best example is adjective: ad (toward) + ject (throw) — "a word thrown next to the noun" — the Roman grammarians named it as an appendage of the noun.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="project" text="project" /> <SpeakButton word="inject" text="inject" /> <SpeakButton word="reject" text="reject" />

### vert/vers: to turn (Latin vertere)

Latin vertere "to turn, to rotate". verse (a line of poetry) literally means "one turn": when the ancients ploughed a field or wrote to the end of a line they had to turn back, so one line was one turn; versus (against) originally referred to the side that "turned toward the other party" in litigation or contest. convert turns the whole thing over (a change of faith, currency conversion), reverse turns it back, and universe is uni (one) + versum (turned into one) — "all things turned into a single whole", the Romans' way of naming the world.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="convert" text="convert" /> <SpeakButton word="reverse" text="reverse" /> <SpeakButton word="universe" text="universe" />

### mit/miss: to send (Latin mittere)

Latin mittere "to send, to dispatch", past participle missus. mission is a task one has been sent out on, message is a letter sent out, and missile is something thrown — before the word came to mean specifically "missile" in the 20th century, it referred to any projectile. permit sends something through, submit pushes something upward from below (extended to "submit", "obey"), and promise is a pledge sent forward in advance.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="mission" text="mission" /> <SpeakButton word="submit" text="submit" /> <SpeakButton word="transmit" text="transmit" />

## Greek roots (7)

### log/logue: word, reason, knowledge (Greek logos)

The Greek word logos carries three senses in one: speech, reason, and calculation. The suffix -logy, "the study of something", comes from here — biology is the study of life, psychology the study of the mind. A famous dispute in Christian translation also turns on this word: the Word in "In the beginning was the Word" at the opening of the Gospel of John is logos in the Greek original, which the Chinese Union Version renders as "the Way". dialogue is speech between two people, monologue speech by one.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="biology" text="biology" /> <SpeakButton word="dialogue" text="dialogue" /> <SpeakButton word="psychology" text="psychology" />

### graph: to write, to inscribe (Greek graphein)

Greek graphein "to inscribe, to draw". photograph (photo = light + graph = write) is a coinage of 1839, literally "an image written in light" — the word is new while the parts are old; telegraph is a letter written to a distance, diagram strings key points together into a figure. The same family includes biography, "a life written down": Greek bios (life) + graphein.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="photograph" text="photograph" /> <SpeakButton word="telegraph" text="telegraph" /> <SpeakButton word="geography" text="geography" />

### bio: life (Greek bios)

Greek bios "life, a lifetime". Besides biology and biography, antibiotic means "against life" — the word antibiotic literally carries its function; symbiosis (sym = together + bios = life) is living together, and ecology and the social sciences each borrowed a layer of its meaning.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="biology" text="biology" /> <SpeakButton word="antibiotic" text="antibiotic" /> <SpeakButton word="symbiosis" text="symbiosis" />

### geo: the earth (Greek gē)

Greek gē "the earth"; the prefix geo- spawned the three brothers geography, geology, and geometry. geometry (gē + metron = measure) literally means "measuring land". Herodotus records that the Nile flooded every year and washed out the field boundaries, so Egyptians had to re-survey the land and surveying flourished; the Greeks called this discipline geometria. The Chinese term for geometry is both a transliteration and a translation, and its source lies on the banks of the Nile.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="geography" text="geography" /> <SpeakButton word="geology" text="geology" /> <SpeakButton word="geometry" text="geometry" />

### phon: sound (Greek phōnē)

Greek phōnē "sound". The telegraph and the telephone appeared one after another in the 19th century, and Greek roots were revived in batches to coin new words: telephone (tēle = far + phōnē = sound), microphone (a device for gathering small sounds), symphony (sounds joined together, an orchestra). The word audio used on this site's [pronunciation](/pronunciation) page comes instead from Latin audire — two "sound" words, one Greek and one Latin.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="telephone" text="telephone" /> <SpeakButton word="symphony" text="symphony" /> <SpeakButton word="microphone" text="microphone" />

### chron: time (Greek khronos)

Greek khronos "time". chronicle is a chronicle written down in time order, chronology is the study of time (a timeline), and chronic originally means "dragging on in time" — in medicine it refers specifically to chronic illness. synchronize lines several times up with each other. The word revived in 20th-century film music and today has become an everyday verb for phones and the cloud.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="chronicle" text="chronicle" /> <SpeakButton word="chronic" text="chronic" /> <SpeakButton word="synchronize" text="synchronize" />

### path: feeling, suffering (Greek pathos)

Greek pathos "feeling, to suffer". sympathy is a shared feeling (pity), empathy is a feeling that goes inside the other person (empathy), and apathy is the absence of feeling (indifference). pathology, "the study of suffering", became the study of disease; pathetic travelled furthest — sliding from "moving" to "pitiful" and on to the colloquial "pathetic". One root, three concentrations of feeling.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="sympathy" text="sympathy" /> <SpeakButton word="empathy" text="empathy" /> <SpeakButton word="pathetic" text="pathetic" />

## Prefix origins (8)

### com-/con-: together (Latin cum)

The Latin preposition cum "with, together with". The most human of its derivatives is companion: com + panis (bread) — "the person you eat a loaf with", so the original sense of companion is a meal friend. combine joins two into one, connect binds things together, compose puts things together.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="companion" text="companion" /> <SpeakButton word="combine" text="combine" /> <SpeakButton word="connect" text="connect" />

### ad-: toward (Latin ad)

Latin ad "toward, to"; on entering English it changes shape to match the following consonant: attract (pulling toward one place), accompany (ac + compan, accompanying a meal friend — bread again), attend (stretching toward something). The nicest story is arrive: ad + ripa (bank) — the original sense of "arrive" is **a ship reaching shore**, a seafaring word that made it ashore.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="attract" text="attract" /> <SpeakButton word="attend" text="attend" /> <SpeakButton word="arrive" text="arrive" />

### ex-: outward (Latin ex)

Latin ex "outward". exit (ex + ire = go) means "to walk out", expose puts something outside. example hides an amphibious word: it comes from Latin exemplum, "a sample taken out"; early on it was borrowed through French as example, and centuries later the same word's colloquial variant essample lost its head and was borrowed again, becoming **sample** — example and sample are one word landing twice.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="exit" text="exit" /> <SpeakButton word="expose" text="expose" /> <SpeakButton word="example" text="example" />

### in-/im-: two in's and one famous accident

Latin has two in's: one means "into" (import, inhabit), the other negation (incorrect). The two are identical in form, so you have to guess from the root. The accident concerns inflammable: in- (intensifying, from the "into" line) + flammable, originally "extremely flammable", but widely misread as "not flammable" — on fire-safety labels this is a deadly misunderstanding. 20th-century US fire authorities simply coined **flammable** for "combustible": an etymological accident bought a safety standard.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="import" text="import" /> <SpeakButton word="incorrect" text="incorrect" /> <SpeakButton word="inflammable" text="inflammable" />

### sub-: under (Latin sub)

Latin sub "underneath". subway is a road under the ground, submarine a vessel under the sea. The Latin ancestor of substance, substantia, comes from substare "to stand beneath" — **the thing that holds up under the phenomena without collapsing**, used by philosophy for "substance" and by chemistry for "material". suburb is the land outside the city (urbs = city); Romans lived in the city and the outskirts were for commoners, so the class divide is still in the word.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="subway" text="subway" /> <SpeakButton word="substance" text="substance" /> <SpeakButton word="suburb" text="suburb" />

### trans-: across (Latin trans)

Latin trans "across, to the other side" and the native English word **through** descend from the same PIE ancestor (\*terə-): one the Latin heir, the other the Germanic heir. transport carries across a distance, translate "carries" meaning to the other side, transform is a shape crossing over. transcend stands highest: leaping outside the boundary.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="translate" text="translate" /> <SpeakButton word="transform" text="transform" /> <SpeakButton word="transcend" text="transcend" />

### pre-/pro-: before, forward (Latin prae / pro)

prae "before" gave pre-: predict (to say beforehand), prevent (to arrive in advance, extended to "prevent"). pro- "forward, in place of" runs two senses side by side: proceed moves forward, while prophet is pro + phanai (to speak) — "one who speaks on God's behalf", so a prophet is not someone who knows first but someone who **speaks first**. protect (pro + tegere = to cover) is a shield held in front.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="predict" text="predict" /> <SpeakButton word="proceed" text="proceed" /> <SpeakButton word="protect" text="protect" />

### re-: back, again (Latin re-)

Latin re- "back, again", its meaning sliding all the way to "against" and even "intense". return turns back, revise looks at it again (extended to "revise"), replace puts it back in place. recall carries both "to summon back" and "to remember": calling a memory "back" — the same word-building logic as "to want it back" in Chinese.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="return" text="return" /> <SpeakButton word="revise" text="revise" /> <SpeakButton word="recall" text="recall" />

## Suffix origins (6)

### -tion: turning a verb into a thing (Latin -tionem)

The Latin fourth-declension noun suffix -tio/-tionem entered English in bulk through French, pressing verbs into nouns meaning "this thing": educate → education, inform → information. It is the highest-visibility suffix in academic vocabulary — its being listed first among noun suffixes in [Roots and affixes](/exam/roots-and-affixes) is no accident.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="education" text="education" /> <SpeakButton word="information" text="information" />

### -able/-ible: deserving to be done (Latin -abilis)

The Latin suffix -abilis (verb stem + able) means "worthy of being…, able to be…": readable means able to be read, portable able to be carried. In Latin it fused with the root before entering English (possible and visible come from the -ibilis branch), and later English took -able and bred its own words (breakable, drinkable). One suffix that, once borrowed, learned to produce native words.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="readable" text="readable" /> <SpeakButton word="possible" text="possible" /> <SpeakButton word="drinkable" text="drinkable" />

### -logy: study (Greek logia)

-logia is the scholasticizing suffix of logos, entering English through French -logie and Latinization: biology, geology, psychology… During the great scientific expansion of the 19th century, -logy became an assembly line for naming new disciplines. It almost always takes a Greek stem — without a Greek stem there is no claim to this "study".

Examples: <SpeakButton word="geology" text="geology" /> <SpeakButton word="technology" text="technology" />

### -cracy/-crat: power (Greek kratos)

Greek kratos "power, rule". democracy (dēmos = people + kratos) is "the power of the people"; the word was born in 5th-century BCE Athens and only came into common English use in the 19th century. autocracy is self-rule; bureaucracy is the rule of paperwork: French bureau (a writing desk) with a Greek suffix, one word spanning two countries.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="democracy" text="democracy" /> <SpeakButton word="bureaucracy" text="bureaucracy" />

### -phobia: fear (Greek phobos)

Greek phobos "fear", also a nickname of the Greek war god. Phobos accompanied his father on campaign, and the moon of Mars still bears his name. -phobia was first a medical suffix (hydrophobia, fear of water — the old name for rabies), was borrowed into everyday speech in the 20th century (claustrophobia, fear of enclosed spaces), and then slid into colloquial "revulsion" (chemophobia and its ilk, alongside the Monday blues). Fear walked down from the altar into the clinical scales.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="claustrophobia" text="claustrophobia" /> <SpeakButton word="hydrophobia" text="hydrophobia" />

### -phone: a sound device (Greek phōnē)

Using phōnē as a suffix is a 19th-century invention: telephone (named in the 1870s), microphone, gramophone — the "device of sound" family, all revivals of Greek parts for the electrical age. The microphone in your phone still carries the Greek spelling of two thousand years ago.

Examples: <SpeakButton word="telephone" text="telephone" /> <SpeakButton word="microphone" text="microphone" />

## Usage notes

- Origin stories are **hooks for memory**, not guarantees of meaning. Some modern senses have drifted away from the etymology (pathetic slid from "moving" to "pathetic"), so learn the common sense first and use the story as an association clue.
- Variants of the same root (duc/duct, vert/vers, mit/miss) change in pronunciation and the spelling follows, while the core meaning stays the same.
- Beyond the 30 entries here, [Roots and affixes](/exam/roots-and-affixes) has the full breakdown chart of 27 roots + 20 prefixes + 18 suffixes; use the two pages together — that one gets you recognizing words, this one makes them stick.
