## Core concepts

### Pronunciation

The English sound system is organized around the 48-phoneme chart current in Chinese textbooks (20 vowels + 28 consonants, British RP); every example word is tappable — see [Pronunciation](/pronunciation).

- The contrast between long and short vowels is a **phonemic contrast**: sheep and ship differ by one sound and that makes two words — it is not a matter of speaking faster or slower. The fastest way to train the ear is with **minimal pairs**: two words that differ by one sound.
- English is a **stress-timed** language: content words stay loud, function words are flattened into weak forms, and /ə/ is the one most often flattened. Stress position also changes part of speech and meaning (ˈrecord = a record / reˈcord = to record).
- The spelling-pronunciation gap has a historical root: the [Great Vowel Shift](/history#the-great-vowel-shift-and-print-1400-1700) of roughly 1400-1700 changed the sounds, and printing after 1476 froze the old spellings. Patterns such as magic e, silent kn-, and the three ways to read -ed are learned per letter group, not by rote word by word.
- The 48 phonemes are a **teaching convention**: tr, dr, ts, dz are strictly consonant clusters rather than independent phonemes; Chinese textbooks list them separately to reach 28 consonants, while formal British phonetic descriptions (such as the Jones chart) do not.

### Vocabulary

The word-building formula is "prefix sets the direction, root sets the core meaning, suffix sets the part of speech"; the quick-memorization breakdown chart is in [Roots and affixes](/exam/roots-and-affixes), the origin stories in [Word roots](/roots).

- **Key point**: French and Latin together make up about half the vocabulary, Greek about a tenth; for the full statistics and the word-formation links, see [Word roots · Two layers of origin](/roots#two-layers-of-origin).
- Variants of the same root (duc/duct, vert/vers, mit/miss) change in spelling to follow the pronunciation, while the core meaning stays; prefixes set the direction (pro- forward, re- back, sub- under, trans- across).
- One semantic field often has **three layers of words**: native words are the most everyday, French words the middle ground, Latin (Greek) words the most written (kingly / royal / regal; ask / question / interrogate). When you meet an unknown word, guess which layer it came from and the register starts to feel right.
- Treat etymologies as hooks for memory, not guarantees of meaning: pathetic slid from "moving" to "pathetic", so the modern sense has already drifted away from the origin — learn the common sense first.
- At the level of sources there are a few verifiable oddities: shirt and skirt are one word arriving twice (native sc- vs Norse sk-); example and sample are one word landing twice; fer and bear are the same PIE word from six thousand years ago.

### History

The 1,600-year overview is in [History](/history); the interactive 33-node version is in the [History timeline](/timeline).

- In 449 three Germanic tribes landed on the island, and the word English comes from the Angles; the 100 most-used words today (the, and, I, to) are almost all native Old English.
- Old Norse borrowings have identifying features: sk- onset (sky, skill), hard g/k (give, get), and batches of everyday words (law, husband, window). The decisive event was a **pronoun blood transfusion**: they, them, their were borrowed as a set from Old Norse — an entire set of core pronouns replaced by a foreign language, rare in the history of language.
- The Norman Conquest of 1066 brought about ten thousand French words, concentrated in upper life: government, law, military, food. Calling the living animal by its native name (cow) and the meat on the plate by its French one (beef) is a living fossil of that vocabulary stratification.
- The actual work of standardization was done by dictionaries: Johnson's *Dictionary* of 1755 holds about 42,000 entries, and Webster's 1828 dictionary fixed the American spellings of the color/center family. English still has no official academy; the split between color and colour was a deliberate act of rule-making in the 19th century.
- After the age of borrowing came the age of coinage: internet, hardware, software, email are almost all rearrangements of native parts, not a fresh wave of foreign borrowings.

### Grammar

The system map and the block-by-block explanations are in [Grammar](/grammar); one-problem-at-a-time breakdowns are across the eleven [Topics](/topic/index) pages.

- Every sentence is one of five basic patterns (SV, SVC, SVO, SVOO, SVOC); deciding which takes only one look at what follows the predicate verb. Separating double objects from object complements takes a single be test: if *him = a fool* holds, it is an object complement; if it does not, a double object.
- Clauses fall into three roles (nominal, attributive, adverbial); picking the connector means looking at **what the clause is missing**: use what/who/which when it lacks a subject or object, that/whether when it lacks nothing. No that after a preposition, no that in a non-restrictive clause.
- The master rule for the three non-finite forms: to do marks purpose and futurity, doing marks active and progressive, done marks passive and perfect. The logical subject of a participle must match the subject of the main clause, or you get a dangling participle.
- The point of the subjunctive is **shifting the tense one step back**; suggest is subjunctive only when it means "to suggest", not when it means "to indicate".
- The skeleton of the passive is a set of be-forms in various tenses plus a past participle, commonly eight combinations; intransitive verbs have no passive, and get + done is a colloquial variant, usually carrying a sense of encountering.
- Modal deduction has a certainty gradient: must (certainly) > may (possibly) > might (even less); negative deduction uses can't, not mustn't.
- Test for cleft sentences: remove It is…that and if the sentence is still complete, it is a cleft.

### Tense

The 16-cell matrix and the nine tenses in detail are in [Tense](/tense); choosing between two side by side is in [Tense comparison](/topic/tense-comparison).

- Tense is time (past, present, future) × aspect (simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive), 16 cells in theory; the weight of everyday use and of exams sits on nine of them. English verbs have **no future-tense form** — will is a modal, and "future" is expressed lexically.
- The core of the present perfect is "an event of the past leaves a result or connection at the present", which makes it mutually exclusive with a definite past time point: *I have seen him yesterday* is an error. The rule of thumb: if the sentence names a specific past time, use the simple past; if it names none and stresses the present relevance, use the present perfect.
- Present instead of future in adverbials (main-future subordinate-present): time and condition clauses take the simple present in place of the future. Know the three exceptions — if meaning "willing", the object clause introduced by whether…or not, and using the present perfect in a condition clause to mark a completed prerequisite.
- Sequence of tenses (indirect speech) shifts the whole clause back one step, but objective truth does not shift (*The teacher said that water boils at 100 degrees*).

## Application scenarios I have recorded

The research methods land in these uses:

- **Tap to hear**: a word looks up real dictionary audio first, and falls back to browser synthesis when the lookup fails or times out; example sentences use only synthesis. Only one sound is kept alive at any moment across the whole site, and tapping the current button again stops it. If the first tap is silent, check the system's English voice packs first or switch to Chrome/Edge. See [Pronunciation](/pronunciation) and the SpeakButton section of the README.
- **Local search**: the site-wide search box tokenizes Chinese per character, so a single character inside a word can still match the body; Western text tokenizes per word. Good for the "I don't remember the whole word" case. See the README.
- **Timeline filtering**: the 33 nodes filter along four threads — speech and writing, vocabulary and etymology, standards and dictionaries, exams and teaching. Selecting only "exams and teaching" makes the process of "institutionalizing proficiency" legible at a glance, from Cambridge's first exam in 1913 through the CEFR, the CET-4/CET-6 exams, and the national equivalency exam. See the [History timeline](/timeline).
- **Page perspectives**: the pronunciation, roots, grammar, and tense pages hold systematic notes; the topic pages keep local problem analyses; the exam and past-paper pages present test materials and assembly experience. The kinds of pages complement each other with no fixed order — see [About](/about).
- **Self-check method**: the confusing-words, non-finite-verb, comparison-structure, and condition-and-concession topic pages share one self-check flow — run each group of entries through the judgment problem, copy the wrong ones into an error notebook, come back the next day and tap the example words to listen again; it counts as passed only when you can hear the difference. See [Confusing words](/topic/confusing-words).
- **Four-step sentence parsing**: find the predicate verb to fix the main-clause skeleton → cut out the clauses → put the modifiers back in place → restore the translation following the pattern. The long-and-complex-sentences page breaks down five sentences layer by layer; the past-paper sentence-pattern version is in [High-frequency grammar and translation](/exam/grammar-and-translation). See [Long and complex sentences](/topic/long-sentences).
- **Three-step word breaking**: find the suffix first to fix the part of speech, then the prefix to fix the direction, and once the root fixes the core meaning put it back into the original sentence to verify. See [Roots and affixes](/exam/roots-and-affixes).
- **Where the topic pages sit**: a full-system page answers "how to study this", while a topic page answers "what exactly is going on in this local spot"; for a local question, entering from a topic page is faster than flipping through a whole chapter. See the [Topics index](/topic/index).

## Pitfalls and misconceptions in practice

### Pronunciation (substitution habits of Chinese speakers)

Six stubborn hard spots with corrective mouth shapes are gathered in [Pronunciation hard spots](/topic/pronunciation-hard-parts), ordered with the ones that most damage comprehension first:

1. /θ/ and /ð/ read as /s/ and /z/ (thank read as sank) — tongue tip lightly against the back of the upper teeth, exaggerate first then pull back in.
2. /v/ read as /w/ (very takes on the onset of "wu") — upper teeth lightly on the lower lip, lips unrounded.
3. /n/ and /ŋ/ not released properly (sin vs sing) — /ŋ/ has the tongue root against the soft palate with no release at the end of the word; singer has one /ŋ/, finger has /ŋg/.
4. Secretly adding a vowel at the end of a word (good picking up "de") — a word-final plosive is just mouth shape, a light release and stop.
5. /e/ stretched into "e" (bed read as /beɪd/) — short, no glide.
6. /æ/ not opened enough (bad closing to bed) — drop the jaw noticeably.

The seventh gate is stress and weak forms: Chinese is syllable-timed, English is stress-timed; if every syllable is read at equal length and equal weight, no amount of correct single sounds will make it sound right.

### Vocabulary and etymology

- inflammable originally means "extremely flammable" but is widely misread as "not flammable" because of the double meaning of in-; 20th-century US fire authorities coined flammable for "combustible", and an etymological accident bought a safety standard. See the "two in's and one famous accident" section of [Word roots](/roots).
- The blank-setting patterns and 14 comparison groups for look-alike, meaning-alike words are in [Confusing words](/topic/confusing-words); the most frequent group is the three senses of lie/lay: the past tense of "to lie down" happens to be lay, so *The book is laying on the desk* is an error.
- Do not use etymology as a synonym dictionary: etymology is a memory hook, and meaning is governed by the modern common sense (see pathetic above).

### Grammar

High-frequency errors by frequency are gathered on the grammar and topic pages; here are the fourteen most easily made:

1. Bare countable singular: *I like dog* means "I like eating dog meat"; to say "I like dogs" it must be *I like dogs*.
2. Clause word order: *Do you know where does he live* is wrong — clauses always take declarative order.
3. Double subjects in a relative clause: the it in *The book that I bought **it** is good* must be deleted.
4. Breaking present-for-future in conditionals: *If it **will rain*** is wrong.
5. although together with but: directly transferring the Chinese "although…but…" is the most frequent error.
6. despite taking a clause: it is a preposition and takes only a noun or a gerund.
7. Adding not to unless: unless already carries negation, and a double negative derails.
8. very modifying a comparative: *much better* is right, *very better* is wrong.
9. Confusing no more than (only, regretfully few) with not more than (at most, neutral).
10. Comparing oneself to oneself: *China is larger than any country in Asia* is wrong — when the subject lies inside the range, add other.
11. Dangling participle: *Walking in the street, a car hit him* (the car was not walking).
12. Stative verbs in the progressive: *I am knowing* is wrong.
13. Present perfect with a definite past time: *I have seen him yesterday* is wrong.
14. Extra prepositions after transitive verbs: discuss about, marry with, enter into the room are all wrong (enter into is used only with abstract nouns such as agreements).

A preposition may not be dropped when a phrasal verb is passivized (*The patients were taken good care **of***); be + past participle has two readings — what can take a by phrase and can turn progressive is the passive voice, and what can do neither is a predicative structure (interested, gone). See [Passive voice](/topic/passive-voice).

### Reference system

- The 48-phoneme chart is a teaching convention, not a phonemic one; when comparing with overseas phonetic materials, the classification of tr/dr/ts/dz differs. See the textbook-convention note in [Pronunciation](/pronunciation).
- Some "rules" set down by 18th-century grammar books (an infinitive cannot be split, a double negative cancels out) are products of prescriptivism, and language facts do not always stand with them. See [History](/history).
- "Shakespeare coined about 1,700 words" is the OED's **first-attested-in-writing** criterion; earliest in his writing does not equal coined by him. See [History](/history).
- The past-paper pages on this site were assembled from public recollections and PDF transcriptions and are **not official editions**; uncertain content carries a uniform marking: **[to be supplied]** (missing), **to be proofread** (translations etc. compiled by this site), **to be verified** (answers derived from context). See [Site notes](/english/Readme) and [Past-paper sources](/exam/papers/sources).
- Assembly criterion: obvious printing errors and OCR damage in transcriptions are cleaned up against the context; grammatical flaws in the reference translations and model essays supplied by sources are kept as they are and not used as model-essay grammar lessons. See the footnotes on each past-paper page.
