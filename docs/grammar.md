# Grammar

Grammar answers one question: **how do words arrange into sentences, and sentences into paragraphs**. I first draw the map of the system, then note the core logic and the trap in each part; tense has its own page (see [Tenses](/tense)), and the exam-side statistics plus breakdowns of long, hard sentences are in [high-frequency grammar and translation](/zh/exam/高频语法与翻译). Key example sentences read aloud on tap.

## The map

```
English grammar
├── Morphology: the job of each word
│   ├── eight word classes + articles
│   └── the verb complex: tense · voice · mood · non-finite forms · modals
└── Syntax: how the jobs combine
    ├── five basic sentence patterns (the frame)
    ├── three clause types (frames stacked on frames: noun · relative · adverbial)
    └── inversion · emphasis · ellipsis (deformations of the frame)
```

When I take apart a long, hard sentence I move down this map: find the finite verb to fix the main clause's frame, then see which slot a clause hangs from, and only then handle the deformations like inversion and ellipsis.

## Eight word classes and the articles

| Class | Job | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun n. | the name of a person, thing or concept | city, honesty |
| Verb v. | action and state, the heart of the sentence | run, seem |
| Adjective adj. | modifies a noun | tall, important |
| Adverb adv. | modifies a verb, an adjective, or the whole sentence | quickly, however |
| Pronoun pron. | stands in for a noun to avoid repetition | he, which |
| Preposition prep. | attaches a noun to the rest of the structure | in, despite |
| Conjunction conj. | joins words and sentences | and, although |
| Interjection int. | stands outside the sentence structure | oh, well |

The articles form a separate group, with three core rules: **a/an marks one of a kind, unspecified** (first mention, any one); **the marks something specific** (known to both parties, mentioned before, or unique — *the sun*); the **zero article** goes with generic plurals and uncountables (*Books are cheap.*). The most common error: a singular countable used generically must carry an article and cannot stand bare — *I like dog* means "I like eating dog", while "I like dogs" is *I like dogs* or *the dog*.

## The five basic sentence patterns

Every English sentence is one of five frames (or an extension of one). To identify the pattern, check exactly one thing: **what follows the finite verb**.

| Pattern | Formula | Example | How to spot it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SV | S + V | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Birds fly." text="Birds fly." /> | an intransitive verb closes the sentence on its own |
| SVC | S + V + C | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The soup tastes good." text="The soup tastes good." /> | after a linking verb (*be* / *seem* / *taste* …) comes a **complement** describing the subject |
| SVO | S + V + O | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She teaches English." text="She teaches English." /> | a transitive verb takes one object |
| SVOO | S + V + O(ind.) + O(dir.) | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He gave me a book." text="He gave me a book." /> | the *give* / *tell* / *show* / *send* family takes two objects, a person and a thing |
| SVOC | S + V + O + C | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="They call him a fool." text="They call him a fool." /> | the object is followed by a complement describing the object |

**Tell double objects from an object complement with *be***: in *He gave me a book*, me ≠ a book (you cannot say *I am a book*), so it is SVOO; in *They call him a fool*, him = a fool (*him is a fool* holds), so *a fool* is an object complement. One copula verb sorts out two patterns.

## The three clause types

A clause is "a sentence inside a sentence". It sorts into three kinds by which job it fills in the larger sentence: noun clauses fill a noun slot, relative clauses fill an adjective slot, adverbial clauses fill an adverb slot.

### Noun clauses

Sentences introduced by *that* / *whether* / a wh-word, which can serve as subject, object, complement or appositive:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="What he said surprised everyone." text="What he said surprised everyone." /> (subject)
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I know that she lives here." text="I know that she lives here." /> (object; *that* is often dropped in object position)
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The news that he won spread fast." text="The news that he won spread fast." /> (appositive, spelling out what the news was)

Two common errors. **Word order**: clauses always take declarative order — *Do you know where does he live* is wrong, *where he live**s*** is right. ***that* vs *what***: *that* only connects and takes no grammatical role; *what* itself functions as the subject or object inside the clause.

### Relative clauses

Introduced by the relative pronouns *who* / *which* / *that*, they modify a noun:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The book that I bought yesterday is good." text="The book that I bought yesterday is good." /> (*that* is the object of *bought*)
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The man who lives next door is a doctor." text="The man who lives next door is a doctor." /> (*who* is the subject of the clause, so its verb agrees with the antecedent)

Four points to keep: **when only *that* is allowed** (the antecedent is *all* / *everything* / an indefinite pronoun, or is modified by an ordinal or a superlative); **not *that* after a preposition** (*in which* is right, *in that* is not); **non-restrictive clauses** (set off by commas, adding commentary) take *which* / *who*, not *that*; and the most common error of all — **the relative word must actually do grammatical work**. *The book that I bought **it** is good* is the classic double-subject of Chinglish, and *it* has to go.

### Adverbial clauses

Nine kinds by meaning (time, place, condition, cause, purpose, result, concession, manner, comparison), each with its own set of connectors. Learn the four high-frequency ones first:

| Type | Connectors | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | when / while / as / before / until | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I was reading when he called." text="I was reading when he called." /> |
| Condition | if / unless / as long as | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="If it rains, we will stay home." text="If it rains, we will stay home." /> |
| Concession | although / though / even if | Although he is young, he is reliable. |
| Cause | because / since / as | Since you are here, let's start. |

**Main future, subordinate present**: adverbial clauses of time and condition use the simple present for future reference, which is what allows the main clause to use the future. *If it **will rain*** is wrong (see the [tense page](/tense) for the detail).

## The three non-finite forms

A verb that is not the predicate has to change shape: the infinitive *to do*, the gerund *doing*, the participle *doing* / *done*. The whole division of labour in one table:

| Form | Core meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| to do | purpose, something still to come | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He went abroad to study." text="He went abroad to study." /> (he went in order to study) |
| doing | active, in progress | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Standing on the hill, we saw the sea." text="Standing on the hill, we saw the sea." /> (we were standing — active) |
| done | passive, completed | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Seen from the hill, the town looks small." text="Seen from the hill, the town looks small." /> (the town is seen — passive) |

Learn the classic pairs: stop **to do** (stop in order to do something else) / stop **doing** (stop what you are doing); remember **to do** (remember to do it — not done yet) / remember **doing** (remember having done it). **The logical subject of a participle must be the same as the main clause's subject.** *Walking in the street, a car hit him* is a dangling participle (the car was not walking) — fix it as *While he was walking, …*

## Mood and the subjunctive

Three moods: indicative (stating facts), imperative (giving orders), subjunctive (stating the counterfactual — a supposition that runs against fact or against what is realistically possible). The rule for the subjunctive is that **the tense steps back one notch**:

| Type of supposition | Clause | Main clause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Against the present | past form (*be* is always *were*) | would + bare infinitive | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="If I were you, I would accept it." text="If I were you, I would accept it." /> |
| Against the past | had done | would have done | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="If he had studied, he would have passed." text="If he had studied, he would have passed." /> |
| Against the future (unlikelily) | should / were to do | would + bare infinitive | If it were to snow tomorrow, we would cancel it. |

*Wish*-clauses step back the same way: I wish I **knew** (present) / I wish I **had known** (past). Another frequent exam point is the object clause after "request" verbs like *suggest* / *insist* / *order* / *demand*, which takes (should) + bare infinitive — He suggested that she **(should) leave** early. Note that *suggest* takes the subjunctive only when it means "to advise"; when it means "to indicate" it does not (The smile suggests that he **is** happy).

## The passive

Passive = **a tense form of *be* + a past participle**, with the receiver of the action as the subject. The eight common cells (matching the 16-cell matrix on the [tense page](/tense)):

| Tense | Passive form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple present | am/is/are done | English is spoken here. |
| Present progressive | am/is/are being done | The road is being repaired. |
| Simple past | was/were done | The bridge was built in 1900. |
| Past progressive | was/were being done | The house was being painted. |
| Present perfect | has/have been done | The work has been finished. |
| Past perfect | had been done | The letter had been sent. |
| Simple future | will be done | A new school will be opened. |
| With a modal | can/must be done | The rule must be followed. |

Two habits worth remembering: intransitive verbs have no passive (you do not say *was happened* for *happen* / *take place*); *get* + done is a colloquial passive variant (He got hired last week), often carrying a sense of being caught up in something or of an outcome.

## Modal verbs

Certainty of inference comes on a gradient: **must (certain) > may (possible) > might (less possible)**; for a negative inference use *can't* (impossible), not *mustn't*. *He must be tired* means "he is certainly tired"; *He can't be tired* means "he cannot possibly be tired". Modal + have done infers about the past: must **have done** (certainly did it), should **have done** (ought to have done it but didn't — carries blame), needn't **have done** (did not need to but did it anyway).

## Inversion, emphasis and ellipsis

- **Partial inversion**: when a negative adverbial is fronted, the auxiliary moves before the subject. Never **have I seen** such a mess. / Not only **does he** sing, but he also dances. / Only then **did I realize** the truth.
- **So / neither inversion**: So do I (me too) / Neither do I (I don't either) — the auxiliary matches the tense of the previous sentence.
- **Cleft sentences**: It is/was + emphasized part + that/who + the rest. Test: if the sentence is still complete after removing *It is … that*, it is a cleft. It was in Paris **that** they met → In Paris they met ✓.
- **Ellipsis**: in a comparative adverbial clause, whatever duplicates the main clause may drop out: She is taller than I (am). / He runs faster than she (does). Adverbial clauses reduced to just their subordinating conjunction (When asked, he nodded = When he was asked) are the norm in writing.
