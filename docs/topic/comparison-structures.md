# Comparison structures

Comparison structures are a high-frequency test point in reading and vocabulary: the hard part is not recognizing more and than, but "which of several comparisons to pick", "whether the two things being compared actually line up", and "whether you catch the no more than kind of negative trap". This record has four parts: positive degree, comparative, superlative, negative traps. For the sentence patterns against past-paper examples, see [High-frequency grammar and translation](/exam/grammar-and-translation).

## Positive degree: the as…as family

Affirmative takes as…as, negative takes not as/so…as, and the multiple goes at the very front:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She is as busy as a bee." text="She is as busy as a bee. (positive degree, equal ratio)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="This room is not so large as that one." text="This room is not so large as that one. (negative positive degree, so is mostly used in the negative)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="This bridge is twice as long as that one." text="This bridge is twice as long as that one. (the multiple goes before as: twice / three times)" />

## Comparative: both sides of than must line up

Comparative + than, and the iron rule is that **the two compared objects must be of the same kind**. population cannot be compared with a country; it must be compared with that of a country:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The population of China is larger than that of India." text="The population of China is larger than that of India. (that stands in for the population — objects aligned)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The roads here are much quieter than those in my hometown." text="The roads here are much quieter than those in my hometown. (those stands in for the roads)" />

"There is a growing…" has two sayings: the comparative doubled (better and better), or more and more + positive degree:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="English is becoming more and more important." text="English is becoming more and more important. (more and more takes the positive degree — do not say more and more important than)" />

"the more… the more…" uses the + comparative in pairs:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The more you practice, the better you get." text="The more you practice, the better you get. (each the carries one comparative)" />

## Modifiers for the comparative: much works, very does not

Before a comparative only degree words of this kind may go — much, far, a little, even, still, a lot — **very cannot modify a comparative directly**:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="This method is far more effective." text="This method is far more effective. (far modifies the comparative)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She looks much better today." text="She looks much better today. (do not say very better)" />

To express "much better" without using a comparative, switch to an ungradable positive-degree word like absolutely fantastic, or just use much better.

## no more than vs not more than: a classic trap pair

| Pattern | Meaning | Tap to hear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no more than | only, merely (with a sense of "too few") | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He has no more than five dollars." text="He has no more than five dollars. (only five dollars — implying it is not enough)" /> |
| not more than | at most, no more than (a neutral upper bound) | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He has not more than five dollars." text="He has not more than five dollars. (not over five dollars — a neutral statement)" /> |

By the same logic, no less than "as many as" carries an exclamation, and not less than "no fewer than" is a lower bound. This pair has set traps in both vocabulary and reading options: when you see no, ask first — "is it complaining about too few, or neutrally capping the amount?"

## Superlative: the range must be circled

A superlative needs a range introduced by in / of / among; when the range is expressed by a personal pronoun, use of:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="It is the largest museum in the country." text="It is the largest museum in the country. (in + a range of place)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="She is the brightest of all the students." text="She is the brightest of all the students. (of + a group of people)" />

There are exceptions to the usual rule of the before a superlative: the the of an adverbial superlative may be dropped (He runs fastest), and when most modifies a positive degree to mean "very" there is no the (a most interesting book).

## One more frequently tested rule: do not compare the incomparable

China is larger than any country in Asia is a wrong sentence — China is itself in Asia, so it becomes comparing itself with itself. Use any other country instead: if the subject is inside the range and you are comparing within it, other is required to set the subject apart; only when the subject lies outside the range can you use any directly:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="China is larger than any other country in Asia." text="China is larger than any other country in Asia. (the subject is inside the range — add other)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Canada is larger than any country in Asia." text="Canada is larger than any country in Asia. (Canada is not in Asia — any directly)" />
