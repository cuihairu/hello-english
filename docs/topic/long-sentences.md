# Long and complex sentences

Getting stuck on reading is usually not the words being unknown but the sentence structure not being seen through. Long and complex sentences have a limited set of patterns: clauses nested in clauses, non-finite forms, inversion, cleft sentences, parenthetical insertions. I have organized a set of parsing steps and broken down five typical sentences layer by layer. For the systematic background of sentence skeletons, see the [grammar page](/grammar).

## The four-step parsing method

1. **Find the predicate verb**: exclude clauses and non-finite forms and fix the main-clause predicate. Once the main clause is found, the skeleton is there.
2. **Cut out the clauses**: where the connector (that / which / when / if…) is, the clause starts and ends — draw brackets at both ends.
3. **Put the modifiers back in place**: prepositional phrases, participial phrases, appositives each go home, and see which word they hang on.
4. **Restore the translation by pattern**: slot into one of the five basic patterns to straighten out the word order (see the five basic patterns section of the [grammar page](/grammar)).

## Five sentences, layer by layer

### Example 1: an appositive clause nested in a long subject

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The fact that people are influenced by advertisements proves that advertising works." text="The fact that people are influenced by advertisements proves that advertising works." />

- The predicate is **proves** (indicative, third person singular); the main-clause skeleton is The fact proves that…
- that people are influenced by advertisements is an **appositive clause of fact**, stating what the fact is.
- that advertising works is an **object clause of proves**.
- Translation: the fact that people are influenced by advertisements proves that advertising works.

### Example 2: a participial phrase as an adverbial

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Having lived in the city for ten years, he knows every corner of it." text="Having lived in the city for ten years, he knows every corner of it." />

- The predicate is **knows**; the main-clause skeleton is he knows every corner.
- Having lived… is a perfect participial phrase as an adverbial of cause, the action preceding the predicate.
- The logical subject must match the main-clause subject: it is he who lived there for ten years, so the participle takes the active perfect form having lived.
- Translation: having lived in the city for ten years, he knows every corner of it.

### Example 3: Not until triggering inversion

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Not until the money ran out did they stop traveling." text="Not until the money ran out did they stop traveling." />

- Two predicates: ran out belongs to the time clause introduced by until, and **did … stop** is the main-clause predicate.
- The negative Not until is fronted to the start of the sentence, and the main clause takes **partial inversion**: the auxiliary did moves ahead of the subject they.
- Restored to normal order: They did not stop traveling until the money ran out.
- Translation: not until the money ran out did they stop traveling.

### Example 4: a cleft sentence

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="It is the ability to do the job that matters, not where you studied." text="It is the ability to do the job that matters, not where you studied." />

- Test: remove It is…that and what remains is The ability to do the job matters — the sentence is still complete, so it is a cleft.
- The emphasized element is the subject the ability to do the job; to do the job is an infinitive as a post-modifier.
- not where you studied is a balancing contrast added on.
- Translation: what matters is the ability to do the job, not where you studied.

### Example 5: three layers nested

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="What surprised me most was that the students who had never spoken in class could present their ideas so clearly." text="What surprised me most was that the students who had never spoken in class could present their ideas so clearly." />

- The main-clause predicate is **was**; the skeleton is What… was that….
- What surprised me most is a **subject clause** (what acts as the subject inside the clause).
- that the students … could present … is a **predicative clause**.
- who had never spoken in class is an **attributive clause** modifying students; the past perfect had spoken marks that "speaking" preceded the past moment of was.
- Translation: what surprised me most was that the students who had never spoken in class could present their ideas so clearly.

## How I practice

I first draw brackets on paper following the four steps, then tap the examples to hear the rhythm — the pauses at clauses can be heard in the audio.
