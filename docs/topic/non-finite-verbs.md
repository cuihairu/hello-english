# Non-finite verbs

The "three non-finite forms" on the [grammar page](/grammar) give each one's own rule; here I organize the decision method when doing, done, and to do land in the same sentence: look at the active or passive relation between the modified verb and its logical subject, and at how the action orders against the predicate.

## The three questions

1. Who performs the action? The logical subject acts actively → doing; it receives passively → done.
2. Does the action come before, after, or at the same time as the predicate? Purpose or futurity → to do; simultaneous with the predicate → doing; earlier than the predicate with emphasis on completion → having done.
3. Where does it stand in the sentence? In the object position it follows the collocation inertia of the preceding verb (enjoy doing / decide to do); after a preposition it is always doing.

## doing vs done: active and passive

A participle as adverbial or attributive — ask first "who did it":

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Entering the room, he found everyone gone." text="Entering the room, he found everyone gone. (he actively 'enters', so doing)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Built in 1990, the bridge is still in use." text="Built in 1990, the bridge is still in use. (the bridge is built, so done)" />

As an attributive the logic is the same: the man **standing** at the gate (currently standing, active), the language **spoken** in this area (it is spoken, passive). When the action precedes the predicate with emphasis on completion, use having done:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Having finished the report, she went home." text="Having finished the report, she went home. (finished first, then went home)" />

## to do vs doing as an object: follow the inertia of the preceding verb

Regulars that take only doing: enjoy, finish, practice, avoid, mind, suggest — <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Would you mind opening the window?" text="Would you mind opening the window?" />

Regulars that take only to do: decide, hope, refuse, agree, plan — <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="They decided to postpone the meeting." text="They decided to postpone the meeting." />

Meaning changing with the form is a fixed piece of drama:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He stopped to smoke." text="He stopped to smoke. (stopped in order to go smoking)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He stopped smoking." text="He stopped smoking. (no longer smoking)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Remember to close the window." text="Remember to close the window. (not yet closed — remember to close it)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I remember closing the window." text="I remember closing the window. (remember having closed it)" />

forget / regret / try work the same way: forget to do is forgetting to do something, forget doing is forgetting that you did it; try to do is making an effort to succeed, try doing is testing how it works.

## After a preposition it is always doing

The object position of a preposition takes only a gerund, no matter how much the sense looks like "should do":

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He is good at repairing bikes." text="He is good at repairing bikes. (repairing after at)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Thank you for helping me with the boxes." text="Thank you for helping me with the boxes. (helping after for)" />

Learn the idioms by their preposition: be interested in doing, look forward to doing, be used to doing (here to is a preposition, distinct from used to do "used to").

## A participle as adverbial: the logical subject must be the main-clause subject

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Walking in the street, I met an old friend." text="Walking in the street, I met an old friend. (I am the one walking, the subject matches — correct)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Walking in the street, a car hit him." text="Walking in the street, a car hit him. (the car was not walking — a dangling participle, wrong)" />

Two fixes for a dangling participle: supply the subject (While he was walking in the street, a car hit him), or hang the participle back onto its real subject. When the subject genuinely cannot show up, an absolute construction is the fallback: Weather permitting, we will go hiking (weather takes itself as subject).

## One more frequently tested rule: verbs of perception and causative verbs

see / watch / hear with an object: taking do is the whole process, doing is what is in progress, done is passive:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I saw him cross the street." text="I saw him cross the street. (the whole process: he crossed)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I saw him crossing the street." text="I saw him crossing the street. (in the act of crossing)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I saw him taken away by the police." text="I saw him taken away by the police. (he was taken — passive)" />

After make / let / have the to is dropped: They made him work all night. Once it turns passive, to comes back: He was made **to** work all night.

Non-finite verbs are tested every year in cloze and translation; a rundown of past-paper sentence patterns is in [High-frequency grammar and translation](/exam/grammar-and-translation), and hands-on work with them cut into long and complex sentences is in [Long and complex sentences](/topic/long-sentences).
