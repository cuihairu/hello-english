# Clause connectors

The [grammar page](/grammar) covers where the three clause types hang and how to take them apart; this record concentrates on choosing the connector inside the clause. Cloze connector blanks and the most frequent type of translation — the relative clause — both test one thing: see what element and what meaning the clause is missing, then pick the word.

## Two questions to settle it

1. **Is anything missing inside the clause?** Subject or object missing → use a word like what, who, which that is both connector and element; nothing missing → use a pure connector like that, whether.
2. **What kind of clause is it?** For a nominal clause, look at the word just before the gap (after a verb it is an object clause, after a noun it may be an appositive clause); for a relative clause, look at the antecedent; for an adverbial clause, look at the logic of the whole sentence.

## that vs which: two identities for relative clauses

that only introduces restrictive clauses, which works restrictive or non-restrictive, and non-restrictive (with a comma) takes only which:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The book that I borrowed is due today." text="The book that I borrowed is due today. (restrictive — that or which both work)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="He passed the exam, which surprised everyone." text="He passed the exam, which surprised everyone. (after the comma only which, referring to the whole matter)" />

When the antecedent is all, everything, anything, or is modified by an ordinal or a superlative, that is the usual choice:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="All that glitters is not gold." text="All that glitters is not gold. (all is followed by that)" />

## what's special right: it connects and also takes a slot

what = the thing(s) that; when the clause is missing a subject or object, only what can step in; that is a pure connector and cannot take a slot inside the clause:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I believe what he said." text="I believe what he said. (said is missing an object — what steps in)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I believe that he is honest." text="I believe that he is honest. (the clause is complete — that only connects)" />

After a preposition, what is taken, that is not: in what he calls "in what he calls…". This one is a regular visitor to cloze connector blanks.

## whether vs if: five places that take only whether

Inside an object clause whether and if are often interchangeable, but in **five places — after a preposition, before an infinitive, as the subject at the start of a sentence, in or not written directly together, and after a noun** — only whether works:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="It depends on whether the weather is fine." text="It depends on whether the weather is fine. (no if after a preposition)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="We discussed whether to go by train." text="We discussed whether to go by train. (no if before an infinitive)" />

## as, the jack of all trades

as can introduce an "as is known" kind of relative clause (referring to the whole sentence), echo such / the same, and also introduce concession (with inversion):

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="As is known to all, the earth goes around the sun." text="As is known to all, the earth goes around the sun. (as refers to the whole main clause — as is known to all)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Such books as you recommended are hard to find." text="Such books as you recommended are hard to find. (such…as echo each other)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Young as he is, he has seen a lot of the world." text="Young as he is, he has seen a lot of the world. (the adjective is fronted before as — concession)" />

Both as and which can refer to the whole sentence: an as clause may come before or after and carries the positive sense of "just as"; a which clause can only follow the main clause. In the past-paper blank "______ is known to all", as is the standard answer.

## whose and of which

whose marks possession, for people or things alike; with things it can also be swapped for the + noun + of which:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The author whose book won the prize is only twenty." text="The author whose book won the prize is only twenty. (his book won)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The house whose roof / the roof of which was damaged has been repaired." text="The house whose roof was damaged has been repaired. (the of which variant is the same in meaning)" />

## One more frequently tested rule: the that of an appositive clause is not surplus

In news that he won came as a surprise, that only connects and takes no slot — that is an appositive clause; if that takes a slot inside the clause (the news that he told me), it is a relative clause, and the that can be swapped for which and dropped when it is the object. The test rule of thumb: **take the content after that out and ask whether the noun in front is still a complete piece of news** — still complete means appositive (explaining the content), incomplete means attributive (limiting the scope).

## How it is tested in past papers

Relative clauses and as clauses are the most frequent patterns in past-paper translation; the comparison table is in [High-frequency grammar and translation](/exam/grammar-and-translation), and the solution for cloze connector blanks is in [High-frequency cloze and completion](/exam/cloze-and-completion).
