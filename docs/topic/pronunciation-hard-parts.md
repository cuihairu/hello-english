# Pronunciation hard spots

For Chinese speakers the hardest part is often not "a sound you have never met" but the substitution habit of "replacing it with some other Chinese sound". This record collects six common pronunciation hard spots, each laid out as "where it usually goes wrong → my adjustment → tap-to-compare". The systematic 48-phoneme chart and the minimal-pairs master table are on the [Pronunciation](/pronunciation) page.

## Hard spot 1: /θ/ and /ð/ read as /s/ and /z/

Chinese has no sound with the tongue tip between the upper and lower teeth, so thank easily comes out as sank and this as zis. The correction is a single action: tongue tip lightly touching the back of the upper teeth, the air coming through the gap, exaggerate first then pull back in.

Keep the division straight: function words (this, that, the, they) mostly take /ð/, content words (think, month, bath) mostly /θ/.

## Hard spot 2: /v/ read as /w/

Chinese w is a rounded sound, so very easily picks up the onset of "wu". The mouth shape for /v/ is upper teeth lightly on the lower lip, the vocal folds vibrating, lips unrounded.

Self-test: <SpeakButton word="vet" text="vet" /> vs <SpeakButton word="wet" text="wet" />; <SpeakButton word="vine" text="vine" /> vs <SpeakButton word="wine" text="wine" />.

## Hard spot 3: /n/ and /ŋ/ not released properly (sin vs sing)

The Mandarin front-back nasal distinction does not help here: English /ŋ/ has the tongue root against the soft palate, with no release at the end of the word and no residual vowel. singer has only one /ŋ/; finger has /ŋg/ — for this syllable-division convention, see the nasals section of the [Pronunciation](/pronunciation) page.

Self-test: <SpeakButton word="sin" text="sin" /> vs <SpeakButton word="sing" text="sing" />; <SpeakButton word="thin" text="thin" /> vs <SpeakButton word="thing" text="thing" />.

## Hard spot 4: a vowel sneaking onto the end of a word

Chinese syllables mostly end in a vowel, so good easily picks up a "de" tail and make a "ke". The correction convention: a word-final plosive is just mouth shape, a light release and stop, with no "uh" padded on after.

Self-test: tap <SpeakButton word="book" text="book" /> <SpeakButton word="good" text="good" /> <SpeakButton word="like" text="like" /> and listen for any extra syllable at the end of the word.

## Hard spot 5: /e/ dragged into "eh"

The short /e/ has no counterpart in Chinese, and bed easily stretches into /beɪd/. The point for /e/ is short, no glide, the mouth half open without spreading.

Self-test: <SpeakButton word="let" text="let" /> vs <SpeakButton word="late" text="late" />; <SpeakButton word="men" text="men" /> vs <SpeakButton word="main" text="main" /> — the latter has a glide, the former has none.

## Hard spot 6: /æ/ not open enough (bad closing to bed)

/æ/ needs the jaw dropped noticeably and the mouth opened wide. Chinese has no front vowel of that openness, so bad and bed blur into one.

Self-test: <SpeakButton word="bed" text="bed" /> vs <SpeakButton word="bad" text="bad" />; <SpeakButton word="men" text="men" /> vs <SpeakButton word="man" text="man" />.

## The last gate: stress and weak forms

English is stress-timed, Chinese is syllable-timed — reading every syllable at equal length and equal weight, even with every individual sound right, will not sound like it. Function words flattened into /ə/ and content words kept loud: for an explanation of this rhythm and the tap comparison of banana and photograph, see [Pronunciation · stress and weak forms](/pronunciation#stress-and-weak-forms).

## How I practice

My order is to work hard spots 3 and 4 first — word endings and /n/-/ŋ/ matter most for being understood; then 1 and 2; and finally 5, 6, and stress and weak forms. For each pair of minimal pairs, first tap and hear the difference, then record yourself and compare on playback — it counts as passed only when someone else can tell which word it is.
