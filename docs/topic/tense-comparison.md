# Tense comparison

The [Tense](/tense) page gives the rules of each of the nine tenses; here I record the decision method I use when two tenses land in the same sentence: put the action on a timeline and see whether it is still connected to "now", and which reference point it comes earlier than. For tense test points and translation statistics from past papers, see [High-frequency grammar and translation](/exam/grammar-and-translation).

## The three questions

1. Does the action take place in the past? Not yet → take the future route (will / be going to / present progressive for something already settled).
2. Is it still connected to now? The result endures, the action continues, and no definite past time point appears → the present perfect family.
3. Is there an earlier layer in the sentence? Yes → that layer takes the past perfect.

## Simple past vs present perfect: a line cut and a line kept

A definite past time point in the sentence (yesterday, in 2010, two days ago) means the line is cut, so use the simple past; no time point and emphasis on the present relevance means the present perfect:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I lost my keys yesterday." text="I lost my keys yesterday. (the line is cut)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I have lost my keys." text="I have lost my keys. (not found yet)" />

For the typical adverbial contrast table, see [Tense · three high-frequency contrasts](/tense#three-high-frequency-contrasts).

## Present perfect vs past perfect: one reference point or two

The present perfect stands at "now" looking back; the past perfect stands at some moment in the past looking at something even earlier. Without that past reference point, the past perfect cannot stand alone:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="The film has started." text="The film has started. (the reference point is the moment of speaking — do not take the wrong one)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="By the time we arrived, the film had started." text="By the time we arrived, the film had started. (arriving is the past reference point, starting is earlier)" />

## Simple past vs past progressive: event and background

In narrative the past progressive lays the background and the simple past delivers the event. The interrupted pattern is a fixed bit of drama:

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I was reading when the phone rang." text="I was reading when the phone rang. (was reading is the background, rang is the interrupting event)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="While she was cooking, he was setting the table." text="While she was cooking, he was setting the table. (while links two parallel backgrounds)" />

## will vs be going to vs present progressive: three futures

| Expression | When the decision is made | Tap to hear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| will | decided on the spot; prediction; promise | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="I will help you with the boxes." text="I will help you with the boxes." /> |
| be going to | planned beforehand; there is evidence | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Look at those clouds. It is going to rain." text="It is going to rain. (the dark clouds are the evidence)" /> |
| present progressive | an arrangement already settled (time and place fixed) | <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="We are meeting the clients at ten." text="We are meeting the clients at ten." /> |

The watershed between will and be going to is when the decision is made: an empty cup on the desk and you say offhand that you **will** get some water; a move arranged last month can only be **we are going to** move.

## Present perfect vs present perfect progressive: result and process

- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Someone has painted the wall." text="Someone has painted the wall. (the wall is painted — look at the result)" />
- <SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="Someone has been painting the wall." text="Someone has been painting the wall. (painting all morning — look at the process)" />

With for / since, or when stressing the ongoing feel of a process, the perfect progressive is more natural: I have been waiting for two hours (still waiting).

## One sentence, three tenses

<SpeakButton kind="sentence" word="When I got home, my husband had already eaten, and he was watching TV." text="When I got home, my husband had already eaten, and he was watching TV." />

- got home: the reference moment of the narrative (simple past).
- had already eaten: before getting home, earlier than the reference point (past perfect).
- was watching TV: the background in progress at the moment of arriving (past progressive).

With the three layers in their places, the logic of tense choice is complete.

## One more frequently tested rule: main future, subordinate present

Time and condition clauses use the simple present in place of the future, and only the main clause takes will; for the three exceptions, see [Tense · main future, subordinate present](/tense#main-future-subordinate-present).
